Scenario 3 — Separate VPS

🟣 VPS B as WireGuard Gateway

A second cheap VPS ($3–5/mo) runs WireGuard as a gateway. Public traffic hits VPS B first. VPS B forwards through encrypted WireGuard tunnel to VPS A. VPS A's real IP is never exposed — only VPS B's IP is public.

Scenario 4 — Commercial VPN

Route all VPS outbound traffic through a commercial VPN (NordVPN, Mullvad, etc.). The VPS connects to NordVPN's servers — outbound traffic gets NordVPN's IP. But inbound connections from users still hit the VPS's real IP directly.

Costs the same as a VPN VPS gateway but gives you almost none of the security benefits. Only useful if you need to anonymize outbound API calls.

🏠 The House Analogy

Your server is a house. Your IP is the address. Your apps are rooms with open doors.

🛡️ S1: No VPN

Your house sits on the street with the address visible. 14 doors are open to the public. You've installed good locks, cameras, and an alarm system — but anyone can walk up and try every door.

🔵 S2: Local WireGuard

Same house, same address on the street — but now you've bricked up 12 of the 14 doors. Only a secret entrance (WireGuard) and the mail slot (SSH) remain. You need a special key to get in. Passersby can see the house but there's almost nothing to try.

🟣 S3: VPS B Gateway

You've moved to a hidden address. Visitors go to a PO box (VPS B) downtown — it receives everything and forwards it to your real house through a secret underground tunnel. Nobody knows where you actually live. If someone attacks the PO box, you replace it in 5 minutes.

🟡 S4: NordVPN

You wear a disguise when you leave the house — nobody at the store knows who you are. But your house address is still on Google Maps, all 14 doors are still open, and anyone can walk right up. The disguise only works when you go out, not when they come in.
